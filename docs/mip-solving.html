1. The problem HiGHS solves

A mixed-integer linear programme (MIP): a linear objective, linear constraints, and some variables that must be integers.

maximise c·x subject to A x ≤ b l ≤ x ≤ u x_j integer for j in J

In our planner almost every variable is binary (0/1): "found a city on site c", "put a Campus on tile t for city c", "build a Mine here". The constraints say what can't happen together: two cities too close, two things on one tile, more than 3 specialty districts. The objective adds up yields.

Without the "integer" line this would be a linear programme (LP), and LPs are easy: solvable in polynomial time, and in practice in seconds even with millions of variables. The integrality requirement makes the problem NP-hard. Everything a MIP solver does is a way of using easy LPs to solve the hard MIP.

Running example, small enough to draw:

maximise z = 5x + 4y subject to 6x + 4y ≤ 24 (constraint 1) x + 2y ≤ 6 (constraint 2) x, y ≥ 0, integer

2. The LP relaxation

Drop "integer" and you get the LP relaxation. Its feasible set is a polytope: the shaded polygon below, bounded by one straight line per constraint. The integer solutions are the dots inside it.

Shaded: LP feasible region. Filled dots: integer points that satisfy both constraints. Dashed lines: objective contours 5x + 4y = const (higher towards the top right). The LP optimum is the corner the contours touch last: (3, 1.5) with z = 21. The best integer point is (4, 0) with z = 20.

Three facts make the relaxation the solver's main tool:

  1. It's a bound. The relaxation allows everything the MIP allows and more, so its optimum (21) is at least as good as the MIP's (20). No integer plan can be worth more than 21. That's the BestBound column.
  2. If the LP optimum happens to be integer, it's the MIP optimum. Nothing better exists, because the bound is achieved.
  3. An LP optimum is always at a corner (vertex) of the polytope. A corner sits where some constraints are tight, and these are often at fractional values. That's why the LP answer is "3 and a half".

The difference between the LP value and the MIP value (21 vs 20 here) is the integrality gap of the formulation. A small gap makes the MIP easy; a big one makes it hard. Most of what follows is about shrinking that gap.

3. The simplex method

The simplex method solves an LP by walking from corner to corner of the polytope along its edges, each step improving the objective, until no neighbouring corner is better.

Start at (0, 0), z = 0. Increasing x pays most (5 per unit), so walk right until a constraint stops you: constraint 1 at x = 4, z = 20. Now increasing y along constraint 1 still pays (+⅔ per unit of y), so walk up the edge until constraint 2 stops you: (3, 1.5), z = 21. No neighbouring corner improves z: optimal. Two steps = two simplex iterations (pivots).
  • LpIters in the HiGHS table counts these pivots. In our shuffle-radius18 log the root LP took 19,301 of them: the polytope has 60,146 dimensions instead of 2, but the idea is the same.
  • Each pivot is a linear-algebra update on a sparse matrix. Its cost grows with the matrix's nonzeros, which is why the log reports that count.
  • Dual simplex. HiGHS mostly uses the dual simplex variant. Its strength is warm starting: when branch and bound adds a bound like y ≤ 1 or a cut, the old optimal corner becomes infeasible, but it's still a very good starting point. The dual simplex repairs it in a few pivots instead of solving from scratch. That's what makes evaluating thousands of nodes affordable.
  • HiGHS also has an interior-point method (IPM), which crosses the inside of the polytope instead of walking its edges. It's sometimes faster for a single big LP, but harder to warm start.

4. Duality: where the bound comes from

Why can the solver claim "no plan beats 21" without trying them all? Because the constraints can be combined into a proof.

Multiply constraint 1 by ¾ and constraint 2 by ½, and add them:

¾ (6x + 4y) ≤ ¾ · 24 → 4.5x + 3y ≤ 18 + ½ ( x + 2y) ≤ ½ · 6 → 0.5x + y ≤ 3 ─────────────────────────────────────────── 5x + 4y ≤ 21

The left side is exactly the objective. So for every feasible point, integer or not, z ≤ 21. The multipliers (¾, ½) are the dual solution, and 21 is the dual bound. Finding the best multipliers is itself an LP (the dual LP), and its optimum always equals the primal LP's optimum: strong duality. The simplex method produces both at once.

So in the log:

  • Primal bound = BestSol = value of a real plan you have in hand (a lower bound on the optimum when maximising).
  • Dual bound = BestBound = value certified by multipliers like these, over all the open branch-and-bound nodes (an upper bound on the optimum).
  • The multipliers also have an economic reading: ¾ is the value of one more unit of constraint 1's right-hand side. These are the shadow prices, or reduced costs for variable bounds. HiGHS uses reduced costs to fix variables: if setting a binary to 1 would cost more than the current gap, it can never be in a better plan, so it's fixed to 0 ("reduced-cost fixing").

5. Branch and bound

The LP gave (3, 1.5). y = 1.5 isn't allowed, and every integer solution has either y ≤ 1 or y ≥ 2. So split the problem in two (branch) and solve the LP of each half. Each half's LP value bounds everything in that half, so halves whose bound can't beat the best plan found so far are thrown away (bound, or "pruned").

Things to notice:

  • Global bound = the best LP value among the open nodes. It only falls as nodes are solved or closed. Incumbent = the best integer plan found. The solver stops when they meet (gap 0) or a limit is hit.
  • A node closes for one of three reasons: its LP is infeasible; its LP solution is integer (a new plan, no need to split further); or its bound is no better than the incumbent.
  • Finding a good incumbent early is valuable: it lets whole subtrees be pruned without ever being explored. That's the job of the heuristics (section 9).
  • With n binaries the full tree has 2ⁿ leaves. For us, 252,327. The solver only gets through it because bounds prune almost everything. The tighter the LP bound, the more gets pruned.

6. Cutting planes

Branching splits the problem. A cut instead adds a new constraint that every integer solution satisfies but the current fractional LP solution violates. It slices off a piece of the polytope that contains no integer points.

A classic way to make one is the Chvátal–Gomory rounding. Take ⅛ of constraint 1 and ¼ of constraint 2:

⅛ (6x + 4y ≤ 24) + ¼ (x + 2y ≤ 6) → x + y ≤ 4.5

x and y are integers, so x + y is an integer, and an integer ≤ 4.5 is ≤ 4. So x + y ≤ 4 holds for every integer solution. The LP point (3, 1.5) has x + y = 4.5, so it's cut off.

Orange triangle: the part of the polytope the cut x + y ≤ 4 removes. It has no integer points inside. After the cut, the LP optimum is the corner (4, 0), z = 20, which is integer: one cut solved the problem with no branching at all.

Real solvers generate many families of cuts. Those HiGHS uses, and where they matter for us:

Cut familyIdeaIn our model
Gomory / MIR (mixed-integer rounding)Round a combination of rows read off the simplex tableau, as above. Generic; works on anything.Everywhere.
CliqueIf binaries are pairwise incompatible, at most one of the whole group can be 1.City spacing, "one thing per tile". See the next section.
Knapsack coverFor Σ aᵢxᵢ ≤ b with binaries: any set of items that can't all fit gives "at least one of them is 0".specialty_limit (at most 3), improvement_limit.
Implied bound"x = 0 forces y = 0", written as y ≤ x.District needs its city.
  • Cuts in the log = how many were generated; InLp = how many are currently kept in the LP. Cuts that stop being useful are dropped, because every row kept makes each simplex pivot more expensive.
  • Cut rounds are what HiGHS was doing from 10.5 s to 27.1 s in our log: 5,277 cuts moved the bound from 8,945 to 8,920. Useful but slow, because each round re-solves a 22,000-row LP.
  • The method that combines cuts and branching, which every modern solver uses, is called branch and cut.

7. Clique cuts and our city spacing

Cities must be more than 3 tiles apart. Take three candidate sites A, B, C that are all within 3 of each other. Written pairwise, as our model first did:

x_A + x_B ≤ 1 x_B + x_C ≤ 1 x_A + x_C ≤ 1
Left: the conflict graph. An edge means "too close to both be cities". Right: what the LP can do. With pairwise rows it sets every site to ½ and collects 1.5 cities' worth of value from a spot where only one city fits. The clique row x_A + x_B + x_C ≤ 1 forbids that.

The LP relaxation with pairwise rows is weak: across a map with hundreds of overlapping sites, it fills the whole map with half-cities and reports a bound far above any real plan. Branch and bound then has to prove it wrong one node at a time. Writing the spacing as clique constraints (one row per maximal group of mutually conflicting sites) makes the LP bound much closer to the truth. HiGHS can find some of these cliques itself (clique cuts), but not reliably on 300+ sites. CLAUDE.md records the result: that change is what made the full maps solvable to optimality.

8. Presolve

Before any LP, the solver simplifies the model. It's cheap and often the most effective single step. Typical reductions, with examples:

ReductionExample
Remove redundant rowsx + y ≤ 5 when x, y are binary: it can never bind. Drop it.
Remove dominated rowsx_A + x_B ≤ 1 when x_A + x_B + x_C ≤ 1 is also present: the clique implies the pair. Drop the pair.
Fix variablesA district whose objective coefficient is negative and that appears only in ≤ rows can be fixed to 0: building it never helps.
Tighten coefficients3x + y ≤ 3 with binaries is equivalent to the tighter x + y ≤ 1. Same integer points, smaller LP polytope.
ProbingTry x = 1 tentatively: if that forces a contradiction, fix x = 0. If it forces y = 1, record the implication.
Detect implied integersA continuous variable that must be integer whenever the others are (our q variables: "1267 implied int." in the log).

In the shuffle-radius18 log presolve removed 28,710 of 51,386 rows. Rows that presolve always throws away are a hint that the model generates redundant constraints. Not generating them saves model-building time in Python and memory.

9. Primal heuristics

Branch and bound alone may take a long time to stumble on a good integer plan. Primal heuristics try to construct one quickly, from the LP solution or from scratch. Each has a letter in the log's Src column:

SrcHeuristicHow it works
JFeasibility jumpLocal search with no LP: start from all zeros and flip variables to reduce constraint violations. Fast and crude. Our log's first plan (worth 207) at 3.2 s.
R, C, ZRounding (randomised, central, ZI)Round the fractional LP solution to integers, repairing violations. Our 5,474 and 7,161 plans.
FFeasibility pumpAlternate between "round the LP point" and "find the LP point closest to that rounding" until they agree.
LSub-MIP (RINS, RENS)Fix every variable on which the LP solution and the incumbent agree, and solve the much smaller MIP that remains, as a mini branch and bound. Expensive but strong. Our 8,810.25 plan at 43.9 s, which closed the gap from 24.6% to 1.25%.
XUser solutionA starting plan supplied by the user (a "MIP start"). scipy.optimize.milp can't pass one; highspy can. A greedy plan as MIP start would give HiGHS a good incumbent at 0 s.

10. Branching, node choice, conflicts

  • Which variable to branch on? The best choice is the one whose two children have the lowest LP values, because that moves the bound most. Strong branching tries candidates' children LPs for real (accurate, expensive). Pseudocosts remember how much the bound moved last time each variable was branched on (cheap estimates). HiGHS mixes the two (reliability branching): strong branching until a variable's pseudocost is trusted. "strong br." in the report counts LP iterations spent on it.
  • Which open node to solve next? The node with the best bound improves the global bound fastest. Diving depth-first finds integer plans fastest. Solvers alternate. InQueue is the number of open nodes; Expl. is the explored share of the tree.
  • Conflict analysis: when a node turns out infeasible, find which few branching decisions caused it and add a constraint forbidding that combination everywhere (as SAT solvers learn clauses). That's the Confl. column.
  • Symmetry: if two variables are interchangeable (e.g. identical candidate sites), the tree contains the same solutions many times, and bounds can't prune the copies. Our model has little symmetry because every tile has its own yields.

11. Strong vs weak formulations

The same set of integer plans can be written with different constraints. They give the same MIP but different LP relaxations. A strong (tight) formulation has an LP polytope close to the convex hull of the integer points: small integrality gap, fast solve. The convex hull itself would be perfect (LP = MIP), but it usually needs far too many constraints.

PatternWeakStrong
Mutual exclusionPairwise x_i + x_j ≤ 1Clique Σ x_i ≤ 1 (section 7)
"District needs its city"Σ_d z_d ≤ M · x (one row, big M)z_d ≤ x for each d (more rows, but at x = ½ each z is capped at ½, not at M/2). Ours, district_needs_city: Σ_t z[d,c,t] ≤ x[c] for each district type d, also the strong form.
Bonus for two things together (y = a·b)y ≤ a, y ≤ b onlyThe same here, because the objective rewards y. With a ≥ ½, b ≥ ½ the LP still collects y = ½ of the bonus: inherent to products, one reason cross-city adjacency made HiGHS slow.
Big-M "if x then constraint"Huge M (1e6)Smallest valid M, or split into simpler rows. Our coefficient range 1–300 shows we have no big-Ms.
Practical rule: when a model gets slow, first look at its LP bound against the best plan (the root gap). A big root gap means the formulation is weak, and better constraints usually help more than more time or a faster solver.

Levers, roughly in order of payoff:

  1. Fewer columns: 50,143 district options are 83% of the model. Pruning options that can't be worth building shrinks every LP.
  2. A MIP start (section 9): the greedy plan or a previous run's plan, via highspy, so pruning starts immediately.
  3. A gap tolerance of 1–2% on big maps: this run would have stopped at 43.9 s with the same plan.
  4. Fewer generated rows: if presolve always removes a family, don't build it.
  5. Another solver (CP-SAT, SCIP) to compare. Multi-threaded heuristics could find the 8,810 plan sooner.

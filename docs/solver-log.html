2. [1–3] Map, candidates, model size

[2/5] Candidates
  312 city sites (best 400 by site value), 0 existing cities, up to 312 new cities
  50,143 district options over 9 types, 1,872 improvement options (6 best per city)

Every district option is a (district type, city site, tile) triple, so it's one binary variable. 312 sites × 9 types × the tiles within 3 of each site gives the 50,143. That's 83% of the variables.

The final line is the size that HiGHS receives: 60,146 columns (variables) and 51,386 rows (constraints). The two biggest constraint families are worth watching. Presolve throws away more than half of the rows (next section), so not_next_to_planned_city is probably mostly redundant.

3. [4] HiGHS header and presolve

[4/5] Solving with HiGHS (time limit 60.0 s, gap 1e-06); it minimises -objective
Signs. scipy.optimize.milp only minimises, so solver.py passes −objective. Every number HiGHS prints is therefore negative: BestSol -8810.25 means a plan worth 8,810.25. For the bound column, "lower" in HiGHS's terms means "upper" in ours.

  • nonzeros: entries in the constraint matrix. Solve time grows with this more than with the row count.
  • Coefficient ranges: the smallest and largest absolute numbers in the constraints (Matrix), the objective (Cost), the variable bounds and the right-hand sides. All of them are within 1–300 here, which is well scaled. Ranges like 1e-6 to 1e+6 would cause numerical trouble and are typical of big-M constraints. We have none.

Presolve simplifies the model before solving: it drops redundant or dominated rows, fixes variables, tightens coefficients. Here it removes 28,710 rows (56%) and no columns. Those rows were pure overhead: building them costs time in Python, and HiGHS discards them anyway.

Solving MIP model with:
   22676 rows
   60146 cols (52327 binary, 0 integer, 1267 implied int., 6552 continuous, 0 domain fixed)

implied int.: continuous variables that presolve proved are integer in any solution anyway (likely the q improvement-bonus variables). continuous: the y neighbour-bonus variables, which are declared continuous because the constraints force them to 0 or 1. That saves branching.

4. [4] The progress table

A MIP solver works with two numbers at once:

  • BestSol (the primal bound, "incumbent"): the best real plan found so far. It only ever improves.
  • BestBound (the dual bound): a proven limit. No plan can be worth more than this. It comes from the LP relaxation (binaries allowed to be fractional), tightened by cuts and branching. It only ever moves down towards BestSol.
  • Gap = |BestBound − BestSol| / |BestSol|. At 0% (within mip_rel_gap) the plan is proven optimal.

BestBound: no plan can be worth more BestSol: best plan found

The proven optimum lies somewhere between the two lines. Where the curves end, the gap is at most 109 points out of 8,810 (1.24%). The plan could be up to 1.24% better than the one we have, and in practice is usually much closer, because the bound tends to be the weaker of the two.

7. [5] Result: score vs objective

  score 4032.8 = site 2643.0 + adjacency 493.0 + improvements 1116.0 - covered tiles 219.2
  35 cities (35 new, 0 existing) · 141 districts (4.03 per city) · 124 improvements (3.54 per city)

The score (4,032.8) and the solver's objective (8,810.25) are different numbers on purpose:

  • The objective includes the bonuses that make the solver build as much as possible: city_bonus 100 × 35 cities + district_bonus 10 × 141 districts = 4,910. evaluate() leaves them out.
  • Score + bonuses = 8,942.8, about 133 more than the objective. evaluate() counts adjacency from every planned district (e.g. "+1 per 2 districts", including other cities' districts). The MIP only sees each district's adjacency to existing districts, its own city centre and the few y/q bonuses. The real plan is slightly better than the solver believed.

The validation lines below the score check rules the model doesn't enforce. In this run, 35 placements sit on a tile next to another city's centre, which that city would claim when founded.

8. What this run tells us

  • Good plan, not proven optimal: at most 1.24% below the optimum. For planning a game, that's fine.
  • The useful work happened at 43.9 s (sub-MIP). With mip_rel_gap = 0.015 the run would have stopped there with the same plan. A 1–2% gap tolerance is a cheap way to cap solve times on large maps.
  • No branching at all suggests the root node is expensive: 5,000+ cuts on a 60,000-column model. Fewer candidates (district options are 83% of the columns) would help more than more time.
  • Presolve removes 56% of the rows. Finding which family they come from (likely not_next_to_planned_city) and not generating them would save build time and memory.
  • This is the kind of instance where a CP-SAT comparison would be informative: mostly binary, and the plan came from a heuristic, not from closing the bound.

Quick checklist for any log: Status in [4] (Optimal?), the final Gap, total time, the line where BestSol made its last big jump, and Proc. (did it branch at all?).
